# 🟢 Part 1: Fresher (0–1 year)

### Q1. What is a behavioral interview and why is it asked? *(Must-know)*
It asks about **past behaviour** ("Tell me about a time when...") because past behaviour predicts future behaviour. Interviewers score communication, ownership, teamwork, learning ability, judgment and values fit. Technical skill alone rarely gets an offer.

### Q2. What is the STAR method? *(Must-know)*
- **S**ituation: short context (1–2 sentences).
- **T**ask: your responsibility or goal.
- **A**ction: what **you** did, step by step (the longest part, ~60%).
- **R**esult: measurable outcome, plus what you learned.
Keep the answer to 1.5–2 minutes.

### Q3. Common mistake: "we" instead of "I"
Say "we" for team context, but describe **your** specific actions with "I". Interviewers hire you, not the team.

### Q4. "Tell me about yourself" *(Must-know)*
Use **Present → Past → Future** in about 60–90 seconds.
> "I am Ritesh Kumar, a full-stack developer with 7 years of experience, currently building React and Node.js services with AI features at [Company]. Earlier I worked on [domain] where I [achievement with number]. I enjoy turning messy requirements into reliable products, and I am now looking for a role where I can own larger systems and mentor others, which is why this role at [Company] interests me."
Do not recite your resume or share personal life details unless asked.

### Q5. Fresher version of "Tell me about yourself"
> "I am Ritesh Kumar, a final-year B.Tech student in Computer Science. I built a MERN e-commerce project with payment integration and deployed it on AWS, and I did a 3-month internship where I fixed 25+ bugs in a React dashboard. I want to start my career as a software engineer where I can learn from experienced teams and ship real features."

### Q6. "Why do you want to join our company?" *(Must-know)*
Show research: product, tech stack, scale, mission, recent news, culture. Connect it to your skills and goals. Avoid "good salary", "brand name" or "work-life balance" as the lead reason.
> "I use your product myself, and I read your engineering blog about moving to event-driven architecture. I want to work on systems at that scale, and my experience with Node.js and Kafka fits the team."

### Q7. "Why should we hire you?"
Three points: (1) skills match the job description, (2) proof with a number, (3) attitude (learns fast, owns work). Keep it confident, not arrogant.

### Q8. Strengths: how to answer
Pick 2–3 strengths relevant to the job and back each with an example. Examples: problem solving, learning quickly, clear communication, ownership, debugging.
> "I learn fast. When our team adopted GraphQL, I built a working prototype in a week and shared a short internal guide that the team still uses."

### Q9. Weaknesses: how to answer *(Must-know)*
Choose a **real, non-critical** weakness, show awareness and the steps you are taking, and give progress.
> "I used to spend too long polishing code before asking for feedback. Now I open a draft pull request early and ask for review on the design, which cut my rework by about half."
Avoid fake weaknesses ("I work too hard") and critical ones ("I miss deadlines").

### Q10. "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?"
Show growth aligned with the company: deeper technical skills, more ownership, mentoring. Do not say "your job" or "running my own company" unless relevant.
> "In five years I want to be a strong senior engineer who owns a product area, designs reliable systems and helps juniors grow."

### Q11. "Why are you leaving your current job?" 
Be positive and forward-looking: growth, scope, learning, new technology. Never badmouth your employer or manager.
> "I have learned a lot here and delivered X. I now want broader ownership and to work with AI-heavy systems, which my current team does not offer."

### Q12. "What do you know about our company?"
Prepare 4 facts: what they sell, customers, tech stack, a recent launch or news, and mission. Check website, LinkedIn, engineering blog, Glassdoor (with caution) and product reviews.

### Q13. "Tell me about a project you are proud of"
Pick one with clear impact. Describe the problem, your role, technologies, challenges and results.
> "I built an order-tracking dashboard (React, Node, PostgreSQL) that cut support calls by 30% because customers could self-serve."

### Q14. "Tell me about a time you failed" (fresher)
Choose a small, real failure with a lesson.
> "In my college project I deployed without testing the payment flow, and it failed during the demo. Afterwards I added automated tests and a staging checklist. In the next project we had zero demo failures."

### Q15. "How do you handle pressure?"
Break work into small tasks, prioritise, communicate early, ask for help, and stay calm.
> "During exam week and a project deadline together, I listed tasks by urgency, finished the critical features first and told my team which items would slip."

### Q16. "Are you a team player?" 
Give an example of collaboration, not only a yes. Mention pair programming, code reviews, helping someone, or resolving a disagreement respectfully.

### Q17. "How do you handle criticism?"
Listen without defending, ask clarifying questions, thank the person, act, and follow up.
> "My senior said my pull requests were too large. I started splitting them to under 300 lines, and review time dropped from two days to a few hours."

### Q18. "What motivates you?"
Solving real problems, learning, seeing users benefit from your work, and working with good teammates. Give an example.

### Q19. "Do you have any questions for us?" *(Must-know)*
Always ask 2–3 thoughtful questions (see Part 6 list). "No questions" signals low interest.

### Q20. "Are you willing to relocate / work in shifts / on-site?"
Answer honestly. If you have constraints, state them politely and early. If flexible, say so and ask about the policy.

### Q21. "Do you have other offers?"
Be honest but tactful: "I am in process with a few companies, but this role is my top choice because of X." Do not invent offers.

### Q22. Salary expectations (fresher)
Research market ranges (verify on levels.fyi, AmbitionBox, Glassdoor). Give a range or say you expect a fair package based on role and market, and that you are open to discuss.

### Q23. "How do you keep learning?"
Courses, documentation, side projects, reading engineering blogs, open source, and teaching others. Give one recent example (e.g. "I built a small RAG chatbot to learn vector databases").

### Q24. "How do you prioritise tasks?"
Impact and urgency, dependencies, effort. Use a simple matrix, clarify with the stakeholder and communicate trade-offs.

### Q25. "Describe your ideal work environment"
Collaborative, with clear goals, feedback culture and room to learn. Stay flexible and avoid listing demands.

### Q26. "What would you do in your first 30 days?"
Learn the product and codebase, set up the environment, meet teammates, fix small bugs, understand processes, and ask questions. Show a plan of listen, learn, then contribute.

### Q27. "What is your biggest achievement?"
Pick one with a number and personal contribution: "Reduced page load from 4 s to 1.2 s by lazy-loading and caching."

### Q28. "How do you handle a task you don't know how to do?"
Break it down, search docs, try a small experiment, ask a teammate after showing what you tried, then document the result.

### Q29. "Do you prefer working alone or in a team?"
"Both: I focus alone for deep work and collaborate for design, reviews and unblocking." Give a balanced answer.

### Q30. Basic etiquette checklist
Be on time (join 5 minutes early), test audio/camera, dress neatly, keep a notebook and resume, speak clearly, thank the interviewer, and send a short thank-you email.

# 🔵 Part 2: Junior (1–3 years)

### Q31. Build a story bank *(Must-know)*
Prepare 6–8 real stories you can reshape for many questions:
1. Delivered under a tight deadline.
2. Fixed a production bug or incident.
3. Disagreement with a teammate.
4. Learned a new technology fast.
5. Mistake or failure and recovery.
6. Improved performance, quality or process.
7. Helped or mentored someone.
8. Handled unclear requirements.
Write each in STAR form with numbers.

### Q32. How to add numbers when you have none
Estimate honestly: time saved, bugs reduced, users affected, request time, tickets closed, lines of code removed, deployments per week. Say "approximately" if unsure.

### Q33. Tell me about a conflict with a coworker *(Must-know)*
Show empathy, direct communication and a solution focus.
> **S:** A backend teammate and I disagreed on API response format. **T:** I needed the contract finalised for a sprint demo. **A:** I asked for a 15-minute call, listened to his constraints, shared my frontend needs, and we agreed on a versioned schema with an example payload in the repo. **R:** Integration finished two days early and we reused the pattern for later APIs.

### Q34. Tell me about a mistake you made
Own it, explain impact, fix, prevent.
> "I ran a migration on staging without a backup and lost test data. I restored from seed scripts in two hours, told the team immediately, and added a pre-migration checklist plus automated backups."

### Q35. Tell me about a tight deadline
Show prioritisation and communication.
> "We had three days to ship a coupon feature. I split it into must-have and nice-to-have, delivered must-have behind a feature flag, and agreed with the product manager to move analytics to the next sprint. We launched on time with no incidents."

### Q36. How do you handle a bug in production? *(Must-know)*
Stay calm → confirm impact → communicate → mitigate first (rollback/feature flag) → find root cause → fix → add test/monitoring → write a short post-mortem (blameless).

### Q37. Tell me about a time you took initiative
> "I noticed our CI took 25 minutes. Without being asked, I cached dependencies and parallelised tests; build time fell to 9 minutes, saving the team about 4 hours per day."

### Q38. Tell me about a time you learned something quickly
Describe the need, how you learned (docs, small prototype, mentor), and what you delivered.

### Q39. How do you handle unclear requirements?
Ask clarifying questions, write down assumptions, confirm with the stakeholder, build a thin slice for early feedback, and iterate.

### Q40. How do you handle disagreement with your manager?
Respect the role, present data and options privately, listen to context, and commit to the final decision even if different from yours. Escalate only for ethics or serious risk.

### Q41. Tell me about feedback that was hard to hear
Share the feedback, your initial reaction (honest), what you changed, and the result.

### Q42. How do you work with someone difficult?
Assume good intent, find shared goals, communicate directly and respectfully, adapt your style, document agreements, and involve a manager only if needed.

### Q43. How do you manage multiple tasks?
Use a task board, daily plan, time boxes and regular updates. Say "no" or renegotiate when capacity is exceeded, showing trade-offs.

### Q44. A time you helped a teammate
Give a concrete example: pair-programmed to unblock a bug, wrote documentation, reviewed code kindly. Result: teammate delivered faster; team knowledge improved.

### Q45. How do you handle code review feedback?
Treat it as learning. Ask questions if unclear, fix quickly, and discuss respectfully when you disagree with technical reasoning. Give reviews that are specific and kind.

### Q46. Describe a time you went above and beyond
Choose an action that benefited the customer or team without being required; avoid examples that suggest overwork or poor boundaries.

### Q47. Why did you choose software engineering?
Honest, short story: curiosity, building things, solving problems. Connect to your current interest (e.g. AI products).

### Q48. How do you stay motivated on repetitive tasks?
Automate when possible, set small goals, learn something from the task, and connect it to the bigger outcome.

### Q49. Tell me about a time you handled stress
Describe the trigger, how you managed it (planning, exercise, breaks, communication) and the outcome. Avoid saying you never feel stress.

### Q50. How do you explain technical things to non-technical people?
Use analogies, avoid jargon, focus on impact, and check understanding.
> "I explained caching to our sales team as 'keeping popular items near the counter so customers wait less'."

### Q51. Describe your work style
Organised, communicates early, writes things down, and asks for feedback. Provide an example.

### Q52. What do you do when you disagree with a technical decision?
Raise concerns with data (benchmarks, risks), propose an alternative or experiment, and accept the team decision. Document the concern if the risk is significant.

### Q53. How do you handle being wrong?
Acknowledge quickly, thank the person who corrected you, fix it and move on. Show an example.

### Q54. How do you handle a missed deadline?
Communicate early with cause and revised plan, deliver the most critical part, and learn what to estimate better next time.

### Q55. How do you balance quality and speed?
Define "good enough" with the stakeholder, protect critical paths with tests, use feature flags, and track tech debt explicitly.

### Q56. How do you onboard to a new codebase?
Read README and architecture docs, run the app locally, trace one feature end to end, fix small bugs, and ask questions after trying.

### Q57. Tell me about a time you worked on a team project
Describe team size, your role, how work was divided, tools used (Git, Jira, Slack), challenges and results.

### Q58. How do you handle remote or hybrid work?
Clear written updates, overlap hours, camera-on for key meetings, documented decisions, and proactive communication. Give an example of delivering well remotely.

### Q59. What would your last manager say about you?
Say something specific and true: "reliable, learns quickly, and could speak up more in large meetings, which I am working on."

### Q60. Do you have any gaps in your resume?
Be honest and brief; explain what you did (learning, family, health, preparation) and show readiness now. See Part 5 and Part 8 for details.

# 🟡 Part 3: Mid (3–5 years)

### Q61. Tell me about a project you owned end to end *(Must-know)*
Cover: problem and stakeholders → options considered → decision → execution (milestones, risks) → results with metrics → lessons.
> "I owned our notification service rewrite. We moved from cron jobs to a queue-based design (SQS + workers), cut delivery delay from 15 min to under 30 s, and reduced failures by 70%."

### Q62. How do you take ownership?
Think beyond your ticket: understand the goal, monitor after release, fix gaps outside your scope when it helps users, and follow through until the outcome is achieved.

### Q63. Tell me about influencing without authority
> "Security required a library upgrade across five services I did not own. I made a short proof of concept showing a vulnerability, offered a script to automate the upgrade, and presented the effort/risk to each lead. Four teams upgraded in two weeks."

### Q64. Tell me about a technical disagreement and how you resolved it *(Must-know)*
Show data-driven discussion: define criteria (cost, latency, team skill), run a small spike, compare, decide, and record in an ADR (architecture decision record).

### Q65. Tell me about a decision you made with incomplete information
State the uncertainty, how you reduced risk (reversible choice, feature flag, small pilot), your reasoning and the result. Mention what you would do differently.

### Q66. How do you estimate work?
Break into tasks, estimate ranges (best/likely/worst), include testing, review and deployment, add buffer for unknowns, and revisit as you learn. Communicate confidence.

### Q67. How do you handle scope creep?
Clarify the goal, show the cost in time/risk, offer alternatives (phase 2, trade something out), get agreement in writing and keep a change log.

### Q68. How do you handle production incidents as an owner? 
Incident commander mindset: restore service first, assign roles, communicate status updates on a schedule, preserve evidence, write a blameless post-mortem with action items and owners.

### Q69. Tell me about a time you improved a process
Describe the pain, your proposal, adoption (pilot, buy-in) and measurable improvement (cycle time, defects).

### Q70. Tell me about a time you reduced technical debt
Quantify the cost of the debt (incidents, slow delivery), negotiate time with product (tie to business value), do incremental refactoring with tests, and show results.

### Q71. How do you mentor juniors?
Set expectations, pair on hard parts, give tasks that stretch, review with explanations, let them struggle a little, and celebrate wins.
> "I mentored two new joiners; both shipped their first feature in three weeks and one now leads a module."

### Q72. How do you give difficult feedback?
Private, timely, specific (situation–behaviour–impact), focused on actions not personality, with a clear ask and follow-up support.

### Q73. How do you say no to a stakeholder?
Understand the underlying need, explain constraints, offer alternatives, and agree on priorities with data. Be respectful and solution-focused.

### Q74. How do you work with product managers and designers?
Understand user problems early, give feasibility feedback, suggest simpler options, share progress openly, and agree on definition of done.

### Q75. Tell me about a time you had to learn a new domain
Ask experts, read docs, shadow users or support, write a glossary, and build small proofs. Show how the learning improved a decision.

### Q76. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a business decision
Present user/technical risk with data, listen to constraints, commit to the decision, and mitigate risks (monitoring, rollback plan). Show maturity, not stubbornness.

### Q77. How do you ensure quality in your work?
Tests (unit/integration), code reviews, CI checks, observability, staged rollouts, and learning from incidents.

### Q78. Describe working with a legacy system
Respect that it works; add characterization tests, make small safe changes, document behaviour, and use the strangler pattern for gradual replacement.

### Q79. How do you handle on-call?
Prepare runbooks, set meaningful alerts, respond calmly, fix root causes to reduce future pages, and hand over clearly.

### Q80. Tell me about a time you used data to make a decision
Share the question, the data source (logs, analytics, A/B test), the analysis and the changed decision with results.

### Q81. How do you handle an underperforming teammate?
Check context, talk privately with empathy, set clear expectations, offer help, and involve the manager if there is no improvement.

### Q82. Tell me about a time you had to adapt to change
Reorg, tech change or shifting priorities: describe your reaction, steps to adapt (learn, ask), and results.

### Q83. How do you balance feature work and maintenance?
Reserve capacity (e.g. 20%) for reliability and debt, tie maintenance to business risk, and make the cost visible.

### Q84. Using AI tools at work *(Must-know in 2025–2026)*
Be transparent and responsible: use AI assistants for boilerplate, tests, exploration and docs; review and test everything; never paste secrets or customer data into unapproved tools; follow company policy; keep core skills sharp.
> "I use Copilot/Claude for scaffolding and test ideas, but I review each change, and I won't share proprietary data with unapproved services."

### Q85. Tell me about a time you used AI to improve work
Give a concrete example with measurable benefit and the checks you added (e.g. LLM-generated test cases reviewed by you, reducing test-writing time by 40%).

### Q86. How do you view AI replacing developers?
Balanced: AI changes the work; engineers who understand problems, design, review, security and domain context become more valuable. Keep learning and use AI to increase leverage.

### Q87. Ethics: a time you raised a concern
Describe the concern (privacy, safety, bias, dark pattern), how you raised it professionally (data, policy references), the outcome, and what you learned. See `../11_Generative_AI/076_AI-Safety-and-Responsible-AI.md`.

### Q88. Handling confidential information
Follow need-to-know, avoid sharing in public channels, use approved tools, and report accidental exposure immediately.

### Q89. How do you handle ambiguity in an AI feature?
Define success metrics and an evaluation set early, start with a baseline, iterate with measured results, and communicate uncertainty (see `../11_Generative_AI/072_LLM-Evaluation-and-Guardrails.md`).

### Q90. What kind of manager do you work best with?
One who sets clear goals, gives regular feedback and trusts you with ownership. Show flexibility and that you can work with different styles.

# 🟠 Part 4: Senior (5–7 years)

### Q91. Tell me about a time you led a project across teams *(Must-know)*
Show stakeholder mapping, clear goals and milestones, communication cadence, risk management, conflict resolution and business outcome.
> "I led a payments migration across four teams: weekly syncs, a shared risk log, a phased rollout by region. Zero downtime, 12% lower fees, completed one week early."

### Q92. Tell me about your biggest technical decision
Context, options (3), criteria, trade-offs, decision process (ADR, review), results after 6–12 months and what you would change.

### Q93. How do you make architectural decisions with the team?
Write a short design doc, invite review, discuss alternatives and risks, run spikes where needed, decide with clear ownership, document in an ADR, revisit when assumptions change.

### Q94. A time you failed as a leader
> "I pushed an aggressive timeline without consulting the team and we burned out two engineers. I reset scope, apologised, adopted estimation sessions with the whole team, and our later releases met commitments."

### Q95. How do you build trust with a new team?
Listen first, learn the context, deliver small wins, be consistent and transparent, give credit, and admit mistakes.

### Q96. How do you raise the quality bar across a team?
Shared standards (lint, tests, review guidelines), pairing, post-mortems, dashboards, and leading by example. Avoid blame; make good practices easy.

### Q97. How do you manage technical risk?
Identify and rank risks, build spikes early, use feature flags and staged rollouts, define rollback plans, monitor and communicate.

### Q98. Describe handling a major outage
Timeline, your role, communications to stakeholders, mitigation, root cause, and systemic fixes (alerts, runbooks, game days). Mention what the customer experienced and how trust was restored.

### Q99. How do you influence product direction as an engineer?
Bring user and technical insights, propose experiments, quantify trade-offs, and partner with product on outcomes rather than just tickets.

### Q100. How do you deal with conflicting priorities from multiple stakeholders?
Make the trade-offs visible, align on company goals, get a decision from the accountable owner, and communicate the outcome.

### Q101. How do you grow other engineers?
Understand their goals, give stretch assignments, provide regular feedback, sponsor them for opportunities and delegate with context, not just tasks.

### Q102. How do you handle a high performer who is hard to work with?
Address behaviour directly with examples and impact, clarify expectations on collaboration, support them, and be willing to act if it continues. Culture is shaped by what you tolerate.

### Q103. How do you hire?
Define the role and criteria, structured interviews with rubrics, work-sample or realistic tasks, calibrate with other interviewers, check for bias, and sell the role honestly.

### Q104. How do you run effective meetings and communicate?
Agenda, owners, decisions recorded, async by default, concise written updates (what, why, risks, asks).

### Q105. How do you handle being the single point of failure?
Document, pair, rotate responsibilities, create runbooks and delegate gradually so the system and the team do not depend on one person.

### Q106. How do you handle a project that is going to be late?
Raise the risk early, present options (scope cut, extra help, new date), recommend one, and keep stakeholders updated.

### Q107. How do you approach cost and business trade-offs in technical work?
Link decisions to money and risk: cloud cost, engineer time, revenue impact, compliance. Propose cheapest solution that meets requirements, with a plan to scale.

### Q108. Tell me about driving a significant improvement with metrics
Baseline, hypothesis, experiment, rollout, measured result (latency, cost, conversion, reliability) and how you sustained it.

### Q109. How do you handle disagreement between two senior engineers on your team?
Clarify the decision criteria, ask both to document trade-offs, timebox a spike or prototype, decide transparently, and ensure both feel heard.

### Q110. How do you deal with burnout (yours or the team's)?
Notice signs, reduce load, clarify priorities, protect focus time, encourage breaks, and talk to the manager early. Set sustainable pace.

### Q111. How do you keep technical depth while leading?
Review designs and critical code, pair occasionally, own a small technical area, read and prototype, and stay close to production metrics.

### Q112. How do you evaluate new technology adoption?
Define the problem, evaluate against criteria (maturity, team skill, cost, security, exit options), run a pilot with success metrics, and decide with a rollback plan. Avoid resume-driven development.

### Q113. How do you ensure responsible AI use in your team?
Policies on data and tools, evaluation gates, red-teaming, human oversight for risky features, monitoring and incident handling (see `../11_Generative_AI/072_LLM-Evaluation-and-Guardrails.md`).

### Q114. How do you handle layoffs or reorg changes in your team?
Be transparent within allowed limits, support affected people, clarify priorities, protect morale, and keep delivering. Show empathy and professionalism.

### Q115. Continuous improvement as a senior engineer
Retrospectives, metrics (DORA), experiments with the process, mentoring, and sharing learnings through docs and talks.

# 🔴 Part 5: Lead / Architect (7+ years)

### Q116. What is your leadership philosophy? *(Must-know)*
Clarity of goals, trust and autonomy with accountability, psychological safety, continuous feedback, growth of people, and results measured by outcomes.
> "I set the 'why' and constraints, let the team choose the 'how', and make failure safe to discuss so problems surface early."

### Q117. Tell me about building or scaling a team
Hiring plan, onboarding, team structure (ownership by domain), rituals, career paths, and metrics of success (delivery, retention, quality).

### Q118. How do you align engineering with business strategy?
Understand company goals, translate to engineering priorities, communicate trade-offs, create a roadmap with measurable outcomes and review quarterly with leadership.

### Q119. How do you manage a team across time zones or vendors?
Overlap hours, written decisions, clear interfaces and ownership, shared dashboards, regular syncs, and trust-building visits or calls.

### Q120. How do you handle underperformance formally?
Document expectations and gaps, give clear feedback, create a time-bound improvement plan with support, follow HR process, and be fair and humane in the outcome.

### Q121. How do you communicate with executives?
Lead with the answer, impact and ask; show options with risks and costs; use simple visuals; avoid jargon; be honest about uncertainty.

### Q122. Tell me about managing a crisis and the stakeholder communication
Describe your communication plan (frequency, channels, owners), what you told customers and leaders, the recovery and the follow-up improvements.

### Q123. How do you build an engineering culture?
Define values with examples, reward behaviours you want (code quality, learning, helping), hold blameless reviews, make decisions visible, and leaders model the behaviour.

### Q124. Diversity, equity and inclusion in hiring and teams
Structured interviews, diverse panels, inclusive job descriptions, mentoring and sponsorship, fair promotion criteria, and psychological safety for all voices.

### Q125. Long-term career vision and the "next step"
Describe a realistic path (staff engineer, architect, engineering manager, AI platform lead), what you want to learn, and the impact you want to make, in a way that fits the company.

# 💻 Part 6: Hands-On Tasks & Templates

### H1. STAR story template
```
Title: <one line>
Situation (1-2 lines): ...
Task (1 line): my responsibility was ...
Action (4-6 bullets, "I ..."): 1) ... 2) ... 3) ...
```

# 🐞 Part 7: Debugging Scenarios

### D1. Your answers are too long and rambling
Use STAR with a timer, pre-write the Result first, cut context to two sentences, and practise aloud.

### D2. Interviewer keeps asking "what did YOU do?"
You are saying "we" too much. Rewrite each story with your own decisions and actions.

### D3. You froze on a failure question
Prepare a real, safe failure story in advance. If you blank, take a breath and say "Let me think of the best example" (a short pause is fine).

### D4. Stories sound fake or perfect
Add honest difficulty: what went wrong, what you were unsure about and what you learned. Real stories have friction.

### D5. You can't remember numbers
Prepare numbers before the interview from performance reviews, dashboards and tickets. Use approximate figures honestly.

### D6. Interviewer asks about a gap you explained badly
Give a calm one-sentence reason, one sentence on what you did, and bring the conversation to current skills. No over-explaining or apologising.

### D7. You badmouthed your previous manager by accident
Recover: "I should say, it was a learning experience; I understand their constraints now and focused on what I could control." Then continue positively.

### D8. Salary question too early
"I'd like to understand the role and scope first. Could you share the budget range for this position?" If pressed, give a researched range.

### D9. Counter-offer from current employer
Consider why you wanted to leave, whether the issues change, and the risks. Decide on long-term growth, not only money. Be professional with both sides.

### D10. Feeling nervous
Prepare, sleep well, breathe, pause before answering, and treat the interview as a conversation. Nervousness is normal and interviewers expect it.

### D11. Culture-fit round feels vague
Ask what the team values and give examples of behaviours: ownership, collaboration, learning, customer focus, with stories.

### D12. Rejected after a good technical round
Common reasons: weak behavioral signals, communication, seniority mismatch or poor motivation. Ask for feedback politely, improve stories, and try again.

# 🧩 Part 8: Tricky Questions

### T1. "What is your biggest weakness?"
Real, manageable, with an improvement plan (see Q9). Not a disguised strength.

### T2. "Why are there gaps in your resume?"
Short, honest explanation and a pivot to skills. Do not hide it or blame others.

### T3. "Why did you change jobs so often?"
Show learning and growth reasons, not conflict. If many short stints, show a pattern of delivery and explain the next role as a long-term choice.

### T4. "Were you fired / laid off?"
Honest and neutral: "The company reduced its team by X%; I delivered A and B and left on good terms." Do not blame; show what you learned.

### T5. "Would you take a lower salary?"
Say you're focused on the right role and growth, but the compensation must be fair for your experience; ask about total package and review cycles.

### T6. "Are you comfortable with a pay cut for a startup?"
Only if you genuinely are. Ask about runway, equity details and growth. State your constraints clearly.

### T7. "Tell me about a time you were overqualified or bored"
Show you added value anyway: automated tasks, mentored others, or asked for bigger scope.

### T8. "What would your enemies say about you?" / "What do your colleagues dislike?"
Mention a mild, real trait with improvement: "I can be too direct in reviews; I now add context and suggestions."

### T9. "Why are you interviewing if you have a good job?"
Growth, new problems and ownership; not because you dislike the current job.

### T10. "How would you handle a manager who gives unethical instructions?"
Clarify, raise concerns with policy and facts, escalate through proper channels (compliance, HR, skip-level), document, and refuse to participate in illegal or harmful acts.

### T11. "Would you work on a project you disagree with morally?"
Be honest about your boundaries (e.g. illegal or harmful use) and show you can work on normal business trade-offs professionally.

### T12. "How do you handle a teammate taking credit for your work?"
Talk privately first with facts, make your work visible through docs and updates, and escalate only if it continues.

### T13. "What if you disagree with the interviewer's technical opinion?"
Respectfully share your reasoning, show openness ("That's a good point; here's a trade-off I see"), and avoid arguing.

### T14. "Tell me something not on your resume"
A genuine strength, hobby or project that shows character (e.g. teaching coding to kids, a side project, a marathon).

### T15. "Do you have questions about the salary or benefits?"
Yes, ask about structure: fixed vs variable, bonus timing, ESOP vesting, insurance, leave, learning budget, hybrid policy.

# 🎤 Part 10: Project / Behavioral Questions

### P1. Full sample STAR answer: production incident
> **S:** On a sale day our checkout API latency rose from 300 ms to 6 s and orders dropped. **T:** As the on-call engineer I had to restore service quickly. **A:** I checked dashboards, saw database CPU at 100%, found a new query missing an index, rolled the release back with a feature flag within 12 minutes, then added the index and a load test in staging. I posted updates every 15 minutes to support and product. **R:** Checkout recovered; estimated loss limited to 25 minutes of degraded traffic. We added a query-review checklist and a latency alert; no repeat in the next 8 months.

### P2. Full sample STAR answer: conflict
> **S:** A designer and I disagreed on a complex animation that slowed low-end phones. **T:** Ship the page on time with good performance. **A:** I measured frame rates on a mid-range Android, shared a short video comparison, and proposed a lighter animation that kept the effect. I invited the designer to test it. **R:** The new version ran at 60 fps, the designer approved, and we added a performance budget to design reviews.

### P3. Full sample STAR answer: leadership without authority
> **S:** Three teams used different logging formats, slowing incident debugging. **T:** I wanted a shared standard though I wasn't their manager. **A:** I wrote a one-page proposal with sample dashboards, built a small shared library, and onboarded one team as a pilot. **R:** Mean time to find root cause dropped from 40 to 15 minutes; the other two teams adopted it within a month.

### P4. Full sample STAR answer: failure
> **S:** I shipped a cache change that served stale prices for 20 minutes. **T:** Fix and prevent recurrence. **A:** I invalidated the cache, informed support, wrote a blameless post-mortem, and added cache-versioning plus tests. **R:** No repeat; the pattern became part of our checklist. I learned to ask "what is the failure mode?" before each cache change.

### P5. Full sample STAR answer: AI feature under ambiguity
> **S:** Product wanted an "AI help assistant" with no clear success definition. **T:** Deliver a useful first version in 6 weeks. **A:** I proposed measurable goals (answer accuracy, deflection), built a 150-question evaluation set with support, shipped a RAG baseline behind a flag, and iterated on retrieval and prompts. I added a guardrail for unsupported questions. **R:** Accuracy improved from 62% to 88% on the eval set; ticket deflection reached 22% in the first month.

### P6. Why do you want to move from service to product (or the reverse)?
Explain a positive pull: ownership of a product, deeper engineering, broader exposure. Avoid disparaging your current type of company.

### P7. Describe your career journey in two minutes
Chronological, with the learning at each step and the thread connecting them (e.g. frontend → full-stack → AI features). End with the next step you want.

# 🔬 Part 12: Internals

### What interviewers actually score
Most companies use a rubric with signals such as **ownership, problem solving, collaboration, communication, learning agility, impact and values**. Each answer is rated (e.g. 1–4) with evidence. Specific details (decisions, numbers, trade-offs) create evidence; vague answers create none.

### Why follow-up questions matter
Interviewers dig 2–3 levels deep ("why that choice?", "what alternative?", "what went wrong?") to verify the story is real and to measure depth. Know your story's details.

### Structured vs unstructured interviews
Structured interviews (same questions, rubric scoring) predict job performance better and reduce bias. Prepare for consistency, not charm.

### Bar raiser / hiring committee process
Some companies (e.g. Amazon) use an independent interviewer and committee review; one strong "no" with evidence can block a hire, so consistent stories across rounds matter.

### Offer process inside a company
Interview feedback → committee/approval → compensation band by level → offer. Negotiation is easiest after the verbal offer and before acceptance; competing offers and clear market data help (verify per company).

### Level calibration
Fresher/junior: execution with guidance. Mid: independent ownership of features. Senior: leads projects and influences design. Lead/staff: multi-team impact, strategy and people growth. Your stories should show the scope of the target level.

### Bias in interviews
Affinity bias, halo effect, recency bias and first-impression bias exist. Clear stories, structured answers and a calm tone help you create comparable evidence.

# 🔮 Part 14: Output & Prediction Questions

### O1.
A story with Situation 30 s, Task 10 s, Action 60 s, Result 20 s = **120 s** total. Good length; Action is 50% of the time (target 55–65%).

### O2.
Fixed salary ₹18 L, variable 10% (₹1.8 L), joining bonus ₹1 L (one-time). Year-1 cash = `18 + 1.8 + 1 = ₹20.8 L`; recurring annual = **₹19.8 L**.

### O3.
Offer A: ₹24 L fixed, no variable. Offer B: ₹20 L fixed + ₹4 L variable (paid 80% on average). B expected = `20 + 3.2` = **₹23.2 L**, which is lower than A in expected cash and has more risk.

### O4.
Notice period 60 days, buyout offered at one month's salary for 30 days. If monthly salary is ₹1.5 L, buying out 30 days costs **₹1.5 L**. Check whether the new employer reimburses it.

### O5.
Interviewer asks "What was the result?" and you answer "It went well." This gives **no evidence**. A better output: "Latency dropped from 2.4 s to 0.6 s and support tickets fell 18%."

### O6.
Story bank coverage: 8 stories covering 12 common themes with overlaps. If every story covers at least 2 themes, coverage = **16 theme slots for 12 themes**, enough to avoid repeating the same story in one interview day.

### O7.
Three interview rounds each ask for a "conflict" story. Using the same story with different details creates inconsistency. Better: prepare **2 conflict stories** and tag which was used in each round.

### O8.
A 10% hike on ₹15 L = ₹16.5 L. A 30% hike = ₹19.5 L. If market rate for the role is ₹20 L, the 10% hike is **₹3.5 L below market**, which supports negotiation with data.

# ⚠️ Part 16: Common Mistakes

- Giving generic answers with no specific example.
- Saying "we" for everything; unclear personal contribution.
- Rambling beyond 3 minutes; no clear result.
- No numbers or outcome.
- Badmouthing a previous employer, manager or colleagues.
- Fake weaknesses or claiming to have no failures.
- Inventing stories or exaggerating titles, impact and years.
- Not researching the company; generic "why us" answers.
- Mentioning salary as the only motivation.
- Arguing with the interviewer instead of discussing trade-offs.
- Lying about notice period, other offers or documents.
- Giving the same story for every question.
- Forgetting to ask questions at the end.
- Pasting company or customer data into AI tools while preparing.
- Ignoring the level: junior-sized stories for a lead role.
- Negotiating only fixed pay and ignoring variable, ESOP and benefits.
- Being negative about layoffs, gaps or job changes instead of showing growth.
